🔄 Matrix Transpose

Concept Overview

The transpose of a matrix $A$, written $A^T$, is formed by flipping the matrix over its main diagonal — that is, swapping its rows and columns. If $A$ is an $m \times n$ matrix, then $A^T$ is an $n \times m$ matrix. Formally, the entry in row $i$, column $j$ of $A^T$ equals the entry in row $j$, column $i$ of $A$: $(A^T)_{ij} = A_{ji}$.

Transposing is a fundamental operation in linear algebra used in everything from solving systems of equations to computing projections, covariance matrices in statistics, and defining symmetric and orthogonal matrices.

Key Formula

Entry-wise definition:

$$(A^T)_{ij} = A_{ji}$$

Double transpose (involution):

$$(A^T)^T = A$$

Transpose of a product:

$$(AB)^T = B^T A^T$$

Transpose of a sum:

$$(A + B)^T = A^T + B^T$$

Practice Problems

Problem 1 (Easy): Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{pmatrix}$. Compute $A^T$.

Step 1: Identify the dimensions. $A$ is a $2 \times 3$ matrix, so $A^T$ will be $3 \times 2$.

Step 2: Apply the rule $(A^T)_{ij} = A_{ji}$. The first row of $A$ becomes the first column of $A^T$, and the second row of $A$ becomes the second column of $A^T$.

Step 3: Write out $A^T$ by placing each row of $A$ as a column:

$$A^T = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 4 \\ 2 & 5 \\ 3 & 6 \end{pmatrix}$$

✅ Answer: $A^T = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 4 \\ 2 & 5 \\ 3 & 6 \end{pmatrix}$, a $3 \times 2$ matrix.

Problem 2 (Medium): Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 3 & 4 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$. Compute $A^T$, then verify that $(A^T)^T = A$.

Step 1: $A$ is $3 \times 2$, so $A^T$ will be $2 \times 3$. Swap rows and columns:

$$A^T = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 4 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$$

Step 2: Verify $(A^T)^T = A$ by transposing $A^T$. $A^T$ is $2 \times 3$, so $(A^T)^T$ is $3 \times 2$. The first row of $A^T$ (which is $[2, 3, 1]$) becomes the first column of $(A^T)^T$, and the second row of $A^T$ (which is $[1, 4, 0]$) becomes the second column:

$$(A^T)^T = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 3 & 4 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$$

Step 3: Compare with the original $A$:

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 3 & 4 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \quad \checkmark$$

✅ Answer: $A^T = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 4 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, and $(A^T)^T = A$ is verified.

Problem 3 (Harder): Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 \end{pmatrix}$. Compute $(AB)^T$ and verify it equals $B^T A^T$.

Step 1: Compute $AB$.

$$AB = \begin{pmatrix} 1\cdot5 + 2\cdot7 & 1\cdot6 + 2\cdot8 \\ 3\cdot5 + 4\cdot7 & 3\cdot6 + 4\cdot8 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 5+14 & 6+16 \\ 15+28 & 18+32 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 19 & 22 \\ 43 & 50 \end{pmatrix}$$

Step 2: Compute $(AB)^T$ by swapping rows and columns of $AB$:

$$(AB)^T = \begin{pmatrix} 19 & 43 \\ 22 & 50 \end{pmatrix}$$

Step 3: Compute $B^T$ and $A^T$:

$$B^T = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 7 \\ 6 & 8 \end{pmatrix}, \quad A^T = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$$

Step 4: Compute $B^T A^T$:

$$B^T A^T = \begin{pmatrix} 5\cdot1 + 7\cdot2 & 5\cdot3 + 7\cdot4 \\ 6\cdot1 + 8\cdot2 & 6\cdot3 + 8\cdot4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 5+14 & 15+28 \\ 6+16 & 18+32 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 19 & 43 \\ 22 & 50 \end{pmatrix}$$

Step 5: Compare: $(AB)^T = \begin{pmatrix} 19 & 43 \\ 22 & 50 \end{pmatrix} = B^T A^T$ ✓

✅ Answer: $(AB)^T = B^T A^T = \begin{pmatrix} 19 & 43 \\ 22 & 50 \end{pmatrix}$. The property is verified.

Key Properties

1. Involution: $(A^T)^T = A$. Transposing twice returns the original matrix.

2. Reversal for products: $(AB)^T = B^T A^T$. The order of multiplication reverses when transposing a product.

3. Linearity: $(A + B)^T = A^T + B^T$ and $(cA)^T = cA^T$ for any scalar $c$.

4. Symmetric matrices: A matrix is symmetric if $A^T = A$, and skew-symmetric if $A^T = -A$.

5. Determinant preserved: $\det(A^T) = \det(A)$, so transposing does not change the determinant.
